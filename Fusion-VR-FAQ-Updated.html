Understanding Digital Museums & Immersive Experiences

25 questions
Q1. What is a Digital Museum?

A Digital Museum in India is a physical museum space that uses immersive technology to transform static exhibits into engaging, multi-sensory visitor experiences. Technologies such as stereoscopic 3D projection, virtual reality, augmented reality, holographic displays, and 3D spatial audio replace traditional glass-case displays — inviting visitors to step inside history, culture, or science through sight, sound, motion, and sensation. Across India, Digital Museums are being adopted by government bodies, heritage trusts, religious institutions, and science centres to modernise visitor engagement and preserve cultural legacy.

Fusion VR's Odyssey of Hanuman 7DX — developed for IITM Pravartak and the Department of Science and Technology, Government of India, and installed in Ayodhya — is one example: a 100-foot curved screen experience with 28K stereoscopic 3D projection, Holophonix spatial audio from France, and 7DX hydraulic motion, delivering several India-first achievements built by a team with more than two decades of experience.

Q2. What is Museum 2.0 and how is it different from a traditional museum?

Museum 2.0 is a philosophy as much as a technology — it reimagines the museum visitor's role from passive observer to active, two-way participant. In a traditional museum, the institution decides what to show and the visitor simply receives it, walking past artefacts behind glass and reading labels. Museum 2.0 breaks this one-way relationship — visitors interact with exhibits, make choices, trigger responses, and shape their own experience in real time through touch, motion, voice, and gesture. The enabling technologies range from touch kiosks and interactive displays, to interactive conversational AI avatars, to fully multisensory immersive environments — all designed to put the visitor in control. The result is a modern museum experience with dramatically higher engagement, longer dwell time, and deeper emotional connection.

Fusion VR has delivered Museum 2.0 experiences across India including the Amma Memorial Museum & Knowledge Park, the Kalaignar Memorial Museum at Marina Beach, the Tamil Nadu Policy & Governance Experiential Centre (TNPAGE), and the Porunai Museum for the Tamil Nadu Archaeology Department.

Q4. What is an Immersive Museum and how is it different from a Digital Museum?

Q5. What does an immersive museum experience actually feel like for a visitor?

Walking into an immersive museum feels less like visiting an exhibition and more like stepping into another world. Instead of standing before glass cases, you are surrounded — a vast screen curves around you, imagery moves in three dimensions, sound travels through the space so a voice or a river or a battle seems to come from a specific direction, and in motion-based experiences the floor or seat moves in sync with what you see. The everyday sense of being in a room fades, and for a few minutes you genuinely feel present in another time or place. Visitors often describe a moment where they forget they are watching a screen at all. In Fusion VR's 7DX VR Boat Ride at the Porunai Museum, for example, visitors feel as though they are physically travelling down an ancient Tamil river — the motion, the spatial sound, and the multisensory effects combining to create the sensation of real time travel into history.

Q6. What is a Virtual Museum — and is it the same as a Digital Museum?

No — a Virtual Museum is not the same as a Digital Museum, and the two are often confused. A Virtual Museum is an online, web-based or app-based museum that visitors access remotely from a computer, phone, or head-mounted VR device — there is no physical space to walk through. A Digital Museum, by contrast, is a physical building or gallery that visitors walk into, where digital and immersive technologies bring the exhibits to life around them. The simplest distinction is location: a Virtual Museum exists on a screen or headset you access from anywhere; a Digital Museum is a real place you travel to and experience in person.

Q7. What is an Experience Centre?

An Experience Centre is a physical space designed to engage visitors with the work, achievements, vision, or offerings of a specific institution, organisation, or brand — using immersive and interactive technology to tell that story compellingly. While a museum primarily preserves and presents heritage, history, or science that exists independently, an Experience Centre is built around the commissioning institution itself — a government department showcasing its policies, a defence organisation presenting its systems, or a corporate brand sharing its journey. The technologies used are often identical to a Digital Museum — immersive projection, interactive displays, AI avatars, and multisensory environments — but the purpose is engagement and communication rather than archival preservation.

Q11. What is digital heritage preservation and how does technology support it?

Digital heritage preservation is the use of technology to capture, archive, recreate, and share cultural heritage — monuments, artefacts, art, traditions, and historical events — in digital form, so it can be protected against loss and experienced by future generations. Technologies such as high-resolution 3D scanning, photogrammetry, the latest Gaussian splatting, digital archiving, and immersive recreation allow fragile artefacts and even vanished or inaccessible sites to be documented with precision and brought to life without any risk to the originals. This serves two purposes at once — safeguarding heritage that may decay, and dramatically widening public access to it.

Q12. What is immersive storytelling in museums and how does it engage visitors?

Immersive storytelling is the art of conveying a museum's narrative not through text panels and labels, but by surrounding visitors in the story itself — using projection, spatial audio, motion, and multisensory effects so they feel they are living the events rather than simply reading about them. Instead of standing before a display about a historical journey, the visitor experiences the journey — the sights, sounds, and sensations that bring it emotionally alive. This works because the human brain remembers experiences far more powerfully than information; emotion and sensory engagement create lasting memory and genuine connection to the subject. Q14. What is immersive theatre and how does it work inside a museum?

An immersive theatre is a museum space where visitors are surrounded by a complete multisensory experience rather than facing a single flat screen. It uses multiple projectors blended seamlessly across a large curved or domed surface to fill the viewer's entire field of vision, combined with sound, motion, and physical effects that engage the whole body.

The key value addition is stereoscopic 3D — it gives the imagery real-world-like depth and perspective, creating a genuine sense of three-dimensional spatial immersion, so objects and environments appear to have volume and distance rather than looking flat.

Unlike a conventional cinema where you watch a framed picture in front of you, an immersive theatre wraps this 3D imagery around and sometimes above you. It combines the visuals with spatial audio, lighting, motion, and multisensory physical effects — such as tactile feedback, wind, drizzle, and aroma or olfactory effects — so the story is felt with the whole body, not just seen.

Fusion VR's Odyssey of Hanuman 7DX at Ayodhya is a landmark example — a 100-foot curved screen with 28K stereoscopic 3D projection across multiple blended projectors, Holophonix spatial audio from France, and synchronised 7DX motion with multisensory effects. Q15. What are interior and exterior 3D Projection mapping, and how are they used in museums and heritage spaces?

3D projection mapping is a technique where digital imagery is precisely shaped to fit the exact contours of a physical surface — a wall, an object, a model, or an entire building — so the surface itself appears to come alive with light, motion, and depth. A key advantage is that it creates the illusion of three-dimensional depth with the naked eye, requiring no 3D glasses or headsets.

It comes in two forms. Interior 3D projection mapping is used inside museum galleries — onto custom-fabricated tabletop miniature models, large-scale built interior sets, dioramas, sculptures, and existing wall murals. Exterior 3D projection mapping is used outdoors, on the façades of monuments, forts, and temples, turning the structure itself into a large-scale storytelling canvas.

In both forms, the physical structure becomes the canvas — a plain miniature model lights up with animated detail, a gallery wall dissolves into a moving story, and a mural comes alive, all without altering the object itself.

Fusion VR specialises in interior 3D projection mapping, with proven capability from custom-fabricated tabletop miniature models to large-scale built interiors and existing wall murals. A flagship example is the custom-designed Chennai urban city tabletop projection mapping at TNPAGE, where a detailed scale model of the city is brought to life through precisely mapped, synchronised visuals.

Q16. Are a sound and light show and projection mapping the same thing?

No — they are related but not the same, though modern shows often combine both.

A traditional sound and light show (also called son et lumière) tells a story using narration, music, and dramatic lighting directed onto a structure or scene — spotlights, coloured washes, and beams illuminate different areas in sync with an audio track. The lighting highlights and dramatises the physical surface, but does not project detailed moving imagery onto it.

Projection mapping goes further — it projects detailed, animated visuals precisely shaped to the contours of the surface, so the structure itself appears to transform, move, and come alive with imagery, not just light.

Q17. What is stereoscopic 3D projection (S3D) and why does it matter for museum experiences?

Stereoscopic 3D (S3D) recreates a virtual world that feels close to the real world by adding the third dimension of depth — the sense of distance a normal flat screen, with only width and height, cannot show. It mirrors how we naturally perceive real physical spaces.

Humans have binocular vision — two eyes positioned slightly apart, each seeing a slightly different view, which the brain combines into a single image with depth. Stereoscopic 3D works the same way: two cameras, like our eyes, and two projectors deliver a separate image to each eye, viewed through special 3D glasses. The brain merges them into one image with real, lifelike depth.

This is why a stereoscopic 3D museum experience feels so real — a river seems to flow toward you, and a historical figure appears to stand in the space with you.

Q19. What are the types of motion simulation experiences and how are they used in museums?

A motion simulation experience uses a computer-controlled moving platform — seats or a floor that tilt, rise, drop, and shake — synchronised precisely with an on-screen journey, so visitors physically feel the movement they see. When the scene climbs, the platform tilts back; when it turns, riders lean into the curve.

Motion platforms come in different types. 3DoF (three degrees of freedom) platforms provide basic, restricted movement, while 6DoF (six degrees of freedom) platforms deliver complete, natural movement across all axes. They are driven by one of three actuator types — pneumatic, electric, or hydraulic. Pneumatic systems are more affordable but prone to jerky motion; hydraulic systems deliver the smoothest, most lifelike movement. The right combination is chosen based on budget, available space, and ease of operation and maintenance.

In museums, motion simulation recreates meaningful journeys — travelling down an ancient river, riding a historic train, or flying over a heritage landscape — transforming passive viewing into a memorable, full-body experience.

Fusion VR's recent installations include the Porunai VR Boat Ride and the TNPAGE 7D. The Odyssey of Hanuman 7DX at Ayodhya uses the most sophisticated system — a 6DoF proportional-controlled-valve hydraulic platform delivering an exceptionally smooth flying experience.

Q20. What is spatial audio, how is it different from surround sound like 5.1 or 7.1, and what role does it play in immersive museum experiences?

Spatial audio is a loosely-used term — many people, and even some consumer products, describe ordinary surround sound as "spatial audio," so it helps to be precise about the difference.

Surround systems like 5.1 or 7.1 use a fixed number of speakers (five or seven, plus a subwoofer), with sound mixed into those specific channels — so audio comes from a handful of fixed directions. True spatial audio goes further: it positions individual sounds as objects anywhere in three-dimensional space, including height and movement, independent of speaker count. A sound can appear precisely above, behind, or move smoothly through the room. This is often achieved using object-based audio and Ambisonics — a full-sphere technique that reproduces sound from every direction, so the soundscape feels completely natural and enveloping.

In a museum, this is essential to immersion. When a temple bell rings from directly above, a chariot rolls past from left to right, and a narrator seems to stand beside you, the story becomes believable in a way visuals alone cannot achieve.

Q21. What are 2D and 3D holographic displays, and how are they used in museums?

A holographic display creates the illusion of a three-dimensional figure or object appearing to float in space, viewable without any headset or glasses — one of the most striking ways a museum can bring a historical figure, deity, or artefact "to life."

It is also one of the most misunderstood technologies, largely due to science-fiction films showing images floating in mid-air. In reality, those are computer-generated effects. For any projection to be seen, its light must hit a surface and reflect back to the eye — light projected into empty air simply travels straight and never returns. True mid-air holograms do not exist physically.

Several approaches are marketed as "holography." The simpler ones — mesh screens, holo-boxes with transparent LCD/LED displays, and holographic fans — are essentially 2D displays showing imagery against a transparent background. The true illusion of 3D depth is achieved only through Pepper's Ghost holography, where imagery is reflected onto an angled surface to appear as a solid, three-dimensional presence. Expensive head-mounted displays such as Microsoft HoloLens and Magic Leap render 3D holographic imagery directly to the wearer's eyes.

Q23. What are AR and MR, and how are they different from VR in museum exhibits?

AR (Augmented Reality) and MR (Mixed Reality) both blend digital content with the real world, unlike VR which replaces the real world entirely. Understanding the difference helps a museum choose the right technology for each exhibit.

Q24. What is the difference between interactive exhibits and fully immersive experiences?

Interactive exhibits and fully immersive experiences are two different but complementary ways a museum engages visitors. The difference lies in how the visitor participates and how completely they are surrounded.

An interactive exhibit responds to visitor input — a touchscreen, a button, a gesture, a sensor-fitted object, or a conversational AI avatar. The visitor actively does something and the exhibit responds. These are typically discrete stations that visitors move between at their own pace.

A fully immersive experience surrounds the visitor completely — through large-format projection, spatial audio, motion, and multisensory effects — transporting them inside a story or place. Here the visitor is enveloped rather than operating a station.

Q154. What is Fusion VR’s approach to energy efficiency and sustainability in museum design?

At Fusion VR we approach energy efficiency and sustainability as a design discipline that runs through every aspect of a digital museum, because these installations run for years and their running cost and footprint matter. We select efficient display, projection, and computing hardware, and build in centralised control that powers galleries up and down on schedule — so systems are never left running when the museum is closed or a gallery is empty.

Efficiency is also engineered into the details. A double-door entrance with a vestibule, or a sliding-door airlock, helps retain conditioned air and cut the air-conditioning power load at busy entrances. In motion-simulation galleries, choosing electric actuators over hydraulic ones for the motion platforms — though the decision is application-specific — can reduce the power load considerably. We weigh choices like these across the whole museum with efficiency and environmental impact in mind.

Sustainability also comes from longevity: because we build durable systems and retain project assets, experiences can be refreshed and upgraded rather than rebuilt from scratch, extending their life and avoiding waste.
